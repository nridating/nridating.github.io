Compatibility across borders: questions for NRI couples

When two people live in different countries, compatibility includes practical questions about where to settle and how to get there. Ask them early.

Why location is a compatibility question

For couples who meet in the same city, where to live is often settled by default. For NRIs it rarely is. A match in Dubai and one in Toronto may share values, faith and humour, yet face a hard decision about whose career moves, which country's visa rules apply, and how far each will be from ageing parents. Treat these as compatibility questions from the start, not paperwork to sort out after the engagement.

Five questions to discuss early

1. Where will we live?

Is either of you committed to a particular country for work, study or family? Is moving back to India a long-term plan for either of you? Many couples find it helpful to describe their ideal location in one year, five years and twenty years.

2. What are our immigration options?

Residency status shapes what is possible. A citizen or permanent resident may be able to sponsor a spouse; someone on a temporary work visa may not. Rules differ by country and change over time, so check official sources rather than relying on friends' experiences. In the UK, for example, the government publishes the requirements for a family visa for partners and spouses.

3. How will our careers fit?

Professional qualifications do not always transfer between countries, and job markets differ. Discuss whose career has more flexibility, whether either of you could work remotely, and how you would manage a period of lower income after a move.

4. How will we care for family?

Parents in India or elsewhere may need more support over time. Talk about visits, money sent home, whether parents might live with you, and how you would share that responsibility between two families in different places.

5. Where and how will we marry?

Some couples marry in India, some in their country of residence, and some in a third country. Where you marry affects paperwork, recognition of the marriage and sometimes visas. If you are a British national marrying abroad, the government's marriage abroad service explains what documents you need for each country.

Cultural compatibility abroad

Living abroad changes how people relate to tradition. One partner may have grown up in Leicester and speak little Hindi; the other may have moved from Hyderabad three years ago. Neither is "more Indian". The useful questions are practical ones:

  • Which festivals and rituals matter to you, and how do you celebrate them where you live?
  • Which languages do you want your children to speak?
  • How do you balance Indian expectations with the culture of the country you live in?
  • What does a typical weekend look like for you?

Making long distance work while you decide

  • Agree how often you will talk and stick to it.
  • Plan the next visit before the current one ends, so there is always something to look forward to.
  • Share the costs of travel fairly.
  • Set a rough timeframe for deciding whether to close the distance, so neither of you feels stuck.

Searching with location in mind

If you are open to a partner in another country, say so in your profile and search widely. If you are not, filter strictly by country to avoid disappointment later. Diaspora-focused services help here: on NRI Matchmaking you can filter by country, city or distance and by resident status, so location expectations are clear before the first message.

Frequently asked questions

Should we discuss visas on a first call?

Not in detail, but it is reasonable to mention where you live and whether you plan to stay there. Detailed immigration questions can wait until you are both interested.

Does it matter where we hold the wedding?

It can. The country where you marry affects legal recognition and paperwork, so check the official requirements before booking anything.

What if neither of us wants to move?

Discuss it openly and early. Sometimes a third location or remote work solves it; sometimes it is a genuine deal-breaker, and it is better to know.

Can my family help with these decisions?

Yes. Parents often have useful views, especially about care and support later in life. The final decision still rests with the couple.
