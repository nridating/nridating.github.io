Writing a matrimonial profile that attracts the right match

Your profile is the first conversation you have with every potential match. A clear, honest one saves time for everyone and brings better introductions.

What a good profile does

Whether you call it a biodata, a matrimonial listing or a profile, it has one job: to help the right person, or their family, decide that a conversation is worth having. It does not need to impress everyone. A profile that is specific and honest will put some people off, and that is exactly what you want, because the people who remain are much more likely to be compatible.

For NRIs, a good profile also answers the questions that living abroad raises: where you live now, how settled you are there, and where you could see yourself in five or ten years.

The essentials to include

  • Basics. First name, age, height if your community expects it, and marital status.
  • Where you live. Country and city, how long you have lived there and your residency status in general terms, such as citizen, permanent resident or on a work visa.
  • Background. Religion, community or mother tongue if they matter to you, and where your family is based.
  • Education and work. Your field and the kind of work you do. A general description is enough; you do not need to name your employer.
  • Lifestyle. Diet, whether you drink or smoke, and how you spend your free time.
  • Family. Parents and siblings in a sentence or two, and how involved they will be.
  • Plans. Where you hope to live, whether you want children, and how you see your career and your partner's fitting together.

Writing about yourself

Most profiles fail because they are generic. "Simple, family-oriented and loves travelling" describes almost everyone. Replace adjectives with small, real details:

  • Instead of "I love travelling", say "I try to visit one new city a year; last year it was Lisbon."
  • Instead of "family-oriented", say "I call my parents in Jaipur every Sunday and visit every Diwali."
  • Instead of "ambitious", say what you are working towards, such as a qualification or a move into a new role.

Write in the first person if you are writing your own profile. If a parent or sibling is writing it, say so at the start, for example "Written by Anjali's brother, with her approval", which builds trust with the reader.

Describing the partner you want

Keep partner preferences short and focused on what genuinely matters. A long list of requirements, such as exact height ranges, specific universities or complexion, makes a profile feel like a job advert and filters out good matches for trivial reasons.

A useful structure is:

  1. Two or three non-negotiables, stated plainly, for example vegetarian, willing to live in Canada, wants children.
  2. Two or three qualities you value, such as kindness, a sense of humour or respect for both families.
  3. Anything you are flexible about, which signals openness.

Matchmaking services that let you mark preferences as must-haves or flexible can then do some of the filtering for you. On NRI Matchmaking, for instance, must-haves are never relaxed when matches are suggested, while other preferences can be.

Photos

  • Use two to four recent photos: one clear portrait and a few natural, full-length or activity shots.
  • Avoid heavy filters, sunglasses in every picture and group photos where it is unclear which person you are.
  • Think about privacy. Services that blur photos for other members or let you choose who sees them give you more control.

Privacy dos and don'ts

  • Do keep your phone number, email, home address and workplace off the profile itself.
  • Do use the service's private messaging until you trust someone.
  • Don't include passport, visa or financial details. A genuine match will not need them at this stage.
  • Don't reuse the same photos you use on professional networking sites, which makes you easy to find elsewhere.

The UK Information Commissioner's Office has guidance for the public on your data rights, including what to do if a service misuses your information.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a matrimonial profile be?

Long enough to give a clear picture, usually 150 to 300 words of description plus the basic details. Readers skim, so put the most important points first.

Should I mention a previous marriage?

Yes. Being open about being divorced or widowed from the start avoids difficult conversations later and attracts people who are comfortable with it.

Is it acceptable to list caste or community?

If it matters to you or your family, you can state it. If it does not, saying that you are open to all backgrounds can widen your options.

How often should I update my profile?

Whenever something important changes, such as a move, a new job or a change in plans, and at least every few months so photos stay recent.
